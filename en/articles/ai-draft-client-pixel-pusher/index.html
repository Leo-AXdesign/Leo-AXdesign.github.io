When a client sends an AI draft and says "just polish this"

More clients and bosses are handing designers drafts made in ChatGPT or Claude and asking only for a finish. Rates go down while the work stays. A diagram of what's actually left, and an order you can use for quotes and conversations.

October 13, 2026 · 4 min read

Translated from the Korean original. Read in Korean

In this article

  1. The "almost done" illusion
  2. Is the work actually shrinking?
  3. How to answer a request to cut the price
  4. So, for now

"I roughed this out with AI. Could you just polish it up?"

Plenty of designers are getting emails like this. Attached is a logo, poster or landing page draft made in ChatGPT or Claude. And there's usually one more line: "Since it's almost done, could you bring the price down a bit?"

On October 12, Fast Company covered this. It interviewed more than a dozen graphic designers, and many described something similar over the past year and a half. Clients send AI drafts and push rates down; at work, bosses hold up AI-made screens and say "build it like this". Some designers who offered opinions were told "ask ChatGPT", and some lost jobs after being told AI could do the work. The piece described designers being turned into "pixel pushers".

First, a disclaimer. This article draws on an interview-based report and a few surveys. There's no precise statistic yet on how common this is. The diagrams and steps below are this site's summary, based on what the designers in the report described.

The "almost done" illusion

To a client, an AI draft looks close to finished. Plausible colors, plausible composition, plausible lettering. But actually using it leaves quite a lot of work.

A few points worth spelling out.

The type usually has to be set from scratch. AI-drawn lettering is a picture. You can't edit it, and non-Latin scripts break often. For a logo, it ends up being redrawn by hand.

There are no files. AI gives you one image. No layers, no vectors, no print resolution. Using it on business cards, signage and social posts is no different from making it again.

The rights check is still open. Can this image be used commercially? Does it resemble another brand's logo or a real person? That's covered in detail in Can you use AI-generated images in client work?

What the AI draft did was settle "this kind of feel". That's useful, but in the whole design job it's the front end.

The work that shrank was mostly simple and fast. Put the other way, the work below the waterline in the diagram, the part that needs judgment and accountability, is still a person's job. The trouble is that clients rarely see it.

How to answer a request to cut the price

"AI did most of it, so it should be cheaper" comes from pricing work by time. The image already exists, so it must take less time. So it's better to answer not with time, but with the work that's left.

What the client saysYou might answer
"It's almost done, just polish it""The direction is good. I'll show you what can be used as is and what needs rebuilding."
"AI made it, so cheaper""I'll quote by the work left, not by hours. Look at the list and drop what you don't need."
"Use this image as the logo""A logo needs to scale and clear trademark checks, so I'll redraw it based on this."
"ChatGPT said to do it this way""I'll try that direction too. Then let's put both side by side and pick."

Examples put together by this site. Adjust the tone to the situation and relationship.

The key is not to dismiss the AI draft. A client showing the direction they want as a picture is actually helpful; it's much faster than explaining in words. Just show, with a list of remaining work, that the picture is a starting point.

It's the same inside a company. When a boss holds up an AI-made screen, take it as a starting point, but separately note "what I decided from here": what you changed and why. Over time, that becomes a record that the designer did more than push pixels.

So, for now

  1. Split the lines on your quote. Instead of "design, one lot", list the remaining work line by line: rights check, resetting type, file prep, per-medium production, number of revision rounds. Clients can see what they're paying for.
  2. Ask where the draft came from. Which AI made it, and whether that service allows commercial use. Keep it in an email so the designer isn't left holding the problem later.
  3. Record what you changed. Save the original AI draft and the final work side by side. It's useful for your portfolio and for the next quote negotiation.
